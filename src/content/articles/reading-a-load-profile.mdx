Every audit starts the same way: a facility manager hands over a stack of nameplate
data and a floor plan, and the temptation is to start sizing things immediately. That
is backwards. The nameplate says what a machine can draw. The meter says what the
building did draw, at 15-minute resolution, for the last twelve months.

## Overnight load is the cheapest thing you will ever find

Plot demand against hour of day and look at the floor of the curve — the level the
facility never drops below. In a single-shift plant that floor should be small: security
lighting, refrigeration, a couple of servers. When it sits at 40% of peak, something is
running that nobody asked to run.

The gap between those two curves is not a projection. It is what the meter recorded
after the compressed air leaks were fixed and the air handlers were put on a schedule.

## Three things the curve gives you for free

1. **Operating hours that are real.** Not the hours in the production schedule — the
   hours the equipment was actually energized.
2. **A weather-dependent fraction.** Regress daily consumption against degree days and
   the slope tells you how much of the load is HVAC and how much is process.
3. **A baseline you can defend.** Measurement and Verification is much easier when the
   pre-period was characterized properly the first time.

## Where it stops helping

Whole-building interval data cannot disaggregate. If two measures affect the same
end use, you will not separate them from the utility meter alone, and you should not
pretend otherwise in the savings calculation. That is what submetering and short-term
logging are for.
